\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:intro}

A web application exposes more to its visitors than the pages it renders. Its HTML source, its
response headers and cookies, and auxiliary files such as \texttt{robots.txt} are delivered to
every client, including one that is preparing an attack. This thesis studies a defence that
turns that exposure against the attacker: an edge proxy in front of the application plants
honeytokens in the traffic it forwards and raises an alert when one of them is used. The
mechanism developed for this purpose is called the Reverse-Proxy Deception Layer (RPDL). The
thesis implements RPDL on four reverse-proxy platforms and measures what it costs on each.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:intro.motivation}

Security testing of a web application begins with information gathering. The OWASP Web
Security Testing Guide instructs a tester to review the comments and metadata in a page's
HTML source, which may reveal internal information that should not be available to an
attacker, and to read \texttt{robots.txt} for paths and functionality that the site does not
link to \cite{owasp2020wstg}. An adversary who reconnoitres the same application has access to
the same artefacts, because they are served to any client that requests them.

Cyber deception exploits this phase of an attack \cite{han2017evaluation}. Spitzner describes a honeypot as an
information system resource whose value lies in unauthorised or illicit use of that resource
\cite{spitzner2003insider}; a honeytoken applies the same idea to a piece of data, such as a
fake credential or a URL parameter that no legitimate workflow uses \cite{han2018deception}. Because a legitimate user has
no reason to touch such an element, an interaction with it is a strong indication of malicious
activity \cite{han2017evaluation}, and deception products have been marketed as intrusion detection with zero false
positives \cite{han2018deception}. Han et al.\ tested this claim for web applications
\cite{han2017evaluation}. Over seven months of production use with 258 regular users, their
deceptive elements raised no false alarm. In a red-team exercise with 150 participants, 84
participants triggered at least one of twelve traps, and 64\,\% of the participants who found
a real vulnerability also triggered a trap. The same study found that 36\,\% of the attackers
who exploited at least one vulnerability did so without triggering any trap, and concluded that
deception is a valuable companion to other detection techniques rather than a standalone
protection \cite{han2017evaluation}.

The practical obstacle is deployment. Kahlhofer and Rass observe that what limits the adoption
of cyber deception is its deployment in real-world software systems, and that the operator
responsible for security usually has access to the built software artefacts but not to their
source code \cite{kahlhofer2024application}. Of the 19 technical methods they review for
deploying application-layer deception without developer involvement, placing a reverse proxy
in front of the application is the one most commonly found in research
\cite{kahlhofer2024application}. Han et al.\ implemented their framework as a transparent
reverse proxy that injects cookies, hidden form fields and \texttt{robots.txt} entries into
responses and removes them from incoming requests before forwarding
\cite{han2017evaluation}. Cloxy couples a reverse proxy with several deception-based defences to
protect legacy web applications that cannot easily be updated \cite{fraunholz2018cloxy}, and
Honey Infiltrator injects honeytokens, such as an HTML comment, into web traffic from a layer-2
network bridge \cite{reti2023honeyinfiltrator}.

A reverse proxy is frequently present already. In Kubernetes, the ingress that connects a
cluster to the outside world is often a reverse proxy such as NGINX or Envoy
\cite{kahlhofer2024application}, and such proxies can be extended with custom code: Envoy, for
example, accepts filters written in C++, Lua or WebAssembly, and developers of custom filters
tend to use Lua or WebAssembly \cite{zhu2023dissecting}. Placing the honeytokens in an edge proxy
that the operator already runs therefore requires no change to the application and no
additional network hop.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:intro.problem}

Running deception logic inside the request path is not free. Kahlhofer and Rass note that the
reverse-proxy pattern often comes with a significant performance overhead and call for research
to reduce it \cite{kahlhofer2024application}. The measurements they refer to come from service
meshes, where Zhu et al.\ report up to 269\,\% higher latency and up to 163\,\% more virtual
CPU cores for their benchmark applications, with the severity depending on configuration and
workload \cite{zhu2023dissecting}. The same study shows that the choice of extension runtime
matters on its own: in Envoy, even a filter that does nothing has a substantial overhead when it
is written in Lua or WebAssembly, and the Lua filter cost three times as much latency and nearly
five times as much CPU as the WebAssembly filter \cite{zhu2023dissecting}. A filter that does
nothing, however, performs no deception. It bounds the cost of hosting an extension, not the cost
of parsing requests for trigger values, rewriting response bodies and recording alerts.

The deception systems discussed above do not close this gap. Cloxy reports that its prototype
increases the delay of communication with the protected web application, a delay its authors
consider tolerable \cite{fraunholz2018cloxy}. The evaluation by Han et al.\ concerns false alarms
and detection accuracy \cite{han2017evaluation}. Neither compares the cost of one deception
mechanism across different proxy technologies, so the choice of edge cannot be made on measured
grounds.


The problem addressed by this thesis is therefore the following. An operator who wants to deploy
honeytokens at the web edge has no measured basis for choosing the edge technology, nor for
estimating what the deception costs in latency, CPU, capacity and functional correctness. The
thesis answers this question for one deception mechanism on four reverse-proxy platforms in a controlled testbed.
All measurements were taken on a single virtualised host serving a static origin, so the
comparison between edges is the claim the results support, while absolute values are not
expected to carry over to other hardware. The effectiveness of honeytokens against real
attackers is outside the scope of this work; detection is tested only against scripted requests
whose expected alerts are known.

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:AB.rq}

The thesis addresses four research questions. Chapter~\ref{chap:AB.results} answers them in this
order.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=3.2em,labelwidth=2.6em,labelsep=0.6em]
  \item[\textbf{RQ1}] How much latency does RPDL add to a request on each edge, and how does the added
    latency change with load?
  \item[\textbf{RQ2}] How much CPU and capacity does RPDL cost on each edge, and where does that cost
    arise?
  \item[\textbf{RQ3}] Does RPDL preserve the responses the edge produced without it, and does it raise
    the alerts it is expected to raise?
  \item[\textbf{RQ4}] To what extent do the answers to RQ1--RQ3 depend on the extension runtime: LuaJIT,
    PUC Lua or WebAssembly?
\end{itemize}

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:intro.contrib}

This thesis makes four contributions.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{RPDL on four reverse-proxy platforms.} One configuration file drives a single deception mechanism
    on OpenResty (nginx with LuaJIT), Envoy with a Lua filter (LuaJIT), Envoy with a WebAssembly
    filter written in Rust against the proxy-wasm interface and executed by V8, and Apache httpd
    with \texttt{mod\_lua} (PUC Lua~5.1). RPDL plants five kinds of honeytoken: HTML comments,
    HTTP response headers, cookies, decoy paths and hidden form fields. It also includes a fake
    SQL-injection trap, in which the edge answers a login endpoint itself with a fabricated
    database error. When a honeytoken is triggered, the edge removes the triggering parameter
    before forwarding the request, records the client address and writes one alert line.
  \item \textbf{Levelling and verified equivalence.} The four implementations were brought to
    one canonical mechanism, with the same parsing, stripping, injection and alert format, and
    their configurations were aligned where differing defaults would otherwise appear as RPDL
    cost, for example in upstream connection reuse. An automated parity check sends thirteen
    requests to every edge and compares the responses, the alert lines and the requests that
    reach the origin against the reference edge, OpenResty. The remaining differences are
    documented.
  \item \textbf{A paired benchmark harness.} Each edge is measured in three tiers in the same
    container: without RPDL (\emph{bare}), with RPDL as deployed (\emph{silent}), and with RPDL
    plus timing instrumentation. The tiers of one replicate are run minutes apart, five
    replicates are taken at 1, 10 and 100 iterations per second (one iteration being nine
    requests), and the probe order follows a Williams design \cite{williams1949designs}. Every
    run is measured on three planes: end-to-end latency from the load generator, CPU time per
    request from the container's control group, and microsecond timers inside the edge. Runs
    that fail explicit validity gates are excluded. Separate experiments measure capacity and
    check functional correctness against the bare tier.
  \item \textbf{Measured costs.} At 1 and 10 iterations per second, RPDL added between 114 and
    218\us{} to the median latency of a GET request, 13--23\,\% of the bare latency, on every
    edge. At 100 iterations per second, three edges stayed between 109 and 136\us, while
    Apache with \texttt{mod\_lua} added 2,174\us{} (+334\,\%); its worker processes crashed with
    segmentation faults 13--16 times per instrumented run at that rate. The marginal CPU cost per
    request was 33\us{} on OpenResty, 41\us{} on Envoy with Lua, 123\us{} on Envoy with
    WebAssembly and 200\us{} on Apache. On all four reverse-proxy platforms, injection corrupted gzip-encoded
    responses and byte-range responses, and both Envoy-based edges failed to deliver an HTML page
    larger than Envoy's 1\,MiB buffer. Under load, every edge raised all expected alerts except
    Apache, whose alert recall was 0.9997 at 10 and 0.9979 at 100 iterations per second.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Thesis Outline}
\label{sec:intro.outline}

Chapter~\ref{chap:background} introduces cyber deception and honeytokens, the reconnaissance
that web honeytokens target, and the request-processing phases of a reverse proxy.
Chapter~\ref{chap:AB.related} reviews prior work on honeytokens and decoys, application-layer
deception without code changes, proxy and service-mesh overhead, and benchmarking methodology.
Chapter~\ref{chap:design} presents the design of RPDL independently of any edge, and
Chapter~\ref{chap:impl} describes its implementation on the four reverse-proxy platforms together with the
levelling that makes them comparable. Chapter~\ref{chap:method} describes the testbed, the
workload, the measurement planes and the criteria for a valid run.
Chapter~\ref{chap:AB.results} presents the results, with one section per research question.
Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion} discusses the results, the choice of an edge for deception, the
threats to validity and the limitations, and Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} concludes and outlines
future work. The appendices contain the configuration reference, the parity-check request set,
instructions for reproducing the benchmarks and additional result tables.

\chapter{Design of the Deception Layer}
\label{chap:design}
The mechanism on a conceptual level, independent of any one edge.

\section{Requirements and Threat Model}
The operator controls the edge but not the application. The attacker is a reconnaissance or
scanning client. Non-goals: blocking traffic, real authentication.

\section{Architecture}
Client $\rightarrow$ edge $\rightarrow$ static origin. One shared \texttt{config.json}, an
in-memory attacker-IP store, and the alert log as RPDL's only output.
\material{\repo{CONTEXT.md} (architecture diagram)}

\section{Honeytoken Kinds}
\subsection{HTML Comments}
Injected before \texttt{</body>}; detected when the trigger keyword appears in the query.
\subsection{HTTP Headers}
A decoy response header; detected when its value is replayed in a later request.
\subsection{Cookies}
\texttt{Set-Cookie} bait; detected on tamper, since a browser replays the value unchanged.
\subsection{Decoy Paths}
Advertised as a hidden link or in \texttt{robots.txt}; any request to the path matches.
\subsection{Form Fields}
A hidden \texttt{<input>} before \texttt{</form>}; detected on tamper.

\section{The Fake SQL-Injection Trap}
The edge answers \texttt{POST /api/login} itself: a fake MySQL error that reflects the payload on a
signature hit, a canned denial otherwise. Signatures are literal strings, not regular expressions.

\section{Detection and Response Pipeline}
Parse once, scan, strip the parameter so the origin never sees it, record the IP, then write one
alert with CR/LF neutralised against log forgery.
